# Non-directional test: Iron Condor on NIFTY, ₹20,000 micro-account

Generated by `research/backtest/chain_replay.py` with
`strategies=CONDOR_STRATEGIES, dte_min=CONDOR_DTE_MIN, dte_max=CONDOR_DTE_MAX`.
Raw output: `chain_replay_NIFTY_iron_condor.json`.

## Why this test: the directional edge doesn't exist, does a non-directional one?

Two independent methods (`chain_replay_NIFTY_findings.md`'s trade-level
replay and `direction_predictability_NIFTY_findings.md`'s ML study) agreed
NIFTY's direction is not predictable from technical features at a ~5-day
horizon. Web research on the alternative — the Volatility Risk Premium
(implied volatility persistently exceeding subsequently realized
volatility, documented since the 1980s, present in ~85–90% of periods on
major indices) — pointed at a genuinely different, non-directional source
of edge: selling defined-risk premium via structures like the Iron Condor,
which already exists in `analysis/spread_selector.py`
(`strategy="IRON_CONDOR"`, `direction="NEUTRAL"`) but had never been
exercised end-to-end before this test.

## A real bug found and fixed along the way

Before any economics could be measured, every Iron Condor candidate on
real recorded chains was rejected `RISK_BELOW_ONE_LOT` — one hundred
percent of them, regardless of how cheap or well-sized the structure
actually was. The cause: `analysis/risk_filters.py`'s one-lot sizing check
assumed every candidate is a debit structure, where the premium paid
equals the max loss per unit, and sized on `net_premium_per_unit` (~₹30–85
for a condor) as if it were that per-unit risk. For a **credit** structure,
the real risk per unit is `width − credit received` (often ~2–3x the
credit itself), not the credit alone — sizing on the wrong, much smaller
number made every condor look unaffordably tiny and rejected it before its
actual economics were ever evaluated.

**Fixed** in `check_candidate()`: a credit structure (`candidate.net_debit
is None`, width set) now sizes on `entry_price=width, stop_loss=credit`
(stop distance = `width − credit`, the real max loss per unit), while a
debit structure's sizing is unchanged. Also fixed: the `RISK_TOO_FAR_FROM_
MONEY` moneyness gate (added for the directional replay) was being applied
to a condor's *long leg*, which is deliberately the far-OTM protective wing
by design — that gate now exempts any `direction="NEUTRAL"` candidate.
Both fixes are covered by new tests in `tests/test_risk_filters.py`, not
just exercised inside this replay script.

## A second, unavoidable constraint: this dataset cannot test the recommended timeframe

Premium-selling strategies are conventionally run at 30–45 days to
expiry — enough time value to decay, still-manageable gamma. **A direct
scan of every recorded (timestamp, expiry) pair in
`data/option_chains.db` found DTE 0–10 is the entire range ever
recorded** — this legacy recorder tracked only the near and next weekly
expiry, never a monthly one. `CONDOR_DTE_MIN`/`CONDOR_DTE_MAX` are set to
7–10, the longest slice the data actually has, not the professionally
recommended range. This is a disclosed data limitation, not a design
choice, and it shows up directly in the result below.

## Result: net loser, and the data constraint visibly caused it

| | |
|---|---|
| Trades closed | 65 |
| Win rate | 36.9% |
| Net P&L | **-₹25,700** |
| Profit factor | **0.4** |
| Cost drag (round-trip costs / gross profit) | **115.5%** — costs alone exceed gross profit |
| Max drawdown | -125.8% of capital (see the account-ruin caveat below) |
| Exit reasons | **100% `TIME_DTE`** — the 50%-of-max-profit target and the 1x-credit stop never fired, even once, across all 65 trades |

Fails the plan's Phase 1 gate (net expectancy > 0, profit factor ≥ 1.3),
same verdict as the directional tests, but the **mechanism is different and
diagnostic**: every single trade rode to the time-based exit because the
7–10 day window this data allows never gave the 50%-of-profit target room
to trigger — that target assumes the 30–45 day timeline the strategy is
designed for, where a meaningful fraction of total theta has decayed well
before the position must be closed. At 7–10 DTE, there simply isn't enough
runway for the intended exit logic to ever engage; the structure gets held
to (near) expiry every time, which is exactly the higher-gamma-risk
behaviour the 50%-target rule exists to avoid.

This matches the web research's own caveat directly: mechanical
(no-adjustment) condor backtests on BankNifty returned as little as
₹4,538 over 3.5 years after costs — thin to negative — with the real edge,
per those sources, coming from **active management** (rolling a threatened
leg), not from mechanically entering and holding. Nothing here modelled
that; this was always going to be the more pessimistic, mechanical-only
answer.

**Caveat on the -125.8% max drawdown:** same as the directional reports —
this replay does not model account ruin, and keeps opening new trades
against the original ₹20,000 regardless of cumulative equity. Read the
rupee/percentage figures as a cost-inclusive per-trade tally, not a
compounding equity curve.

## What would actually test this properly

1. **A dataset with real 30–45 DTE history.** The SENSEX recorder now
   running (`scripts/record_chains.py --symbols SENSEX --exchange BFO`,
   started for the earlier directional findings) currently tracks 3
   expiries per cycle — worth checking, once enough history accumulates,
   whether that includes a monthly expiry, unlike this legacy NIFTY
   recorder.
2. **Active leg management**, not just a flat target/stop — the literature
   is consistent that this is where a condor's real edge lives. Not built
   here; a materially larger piece of work (tracking a threatened short
   leg's distance from spot day-to-day and rolling it, not just
   entering/exiting the whole structure).
3. **Wing-width tuning.** `SIGNAL_MAX_WIDTH_STEPS` (default 4) was left at
   its directional-strategy default; a condor's wing width is a real,
   untuned parameter here.

## Recommendation

Same as the directional findings: **do not build the execution engine on
either strategy tested so far.** Both a directional debit-vertical approach
and a mechanical, short-DTE Iron Condor fail the same gate on the same
real data, for different, now well-understood reasons — one lacks a
direction signal, the other lacks the timeframe and the active management
the strategy actually needs. The path forward is either (a) get real
30–45 DTE chain history and re-test with proper active management, or (b)
treat this as evidence that a small, ₹20,000 account may not have a
rule-based options edge available to it at all on the timeframes testable
so far, and reconsider the goal.
